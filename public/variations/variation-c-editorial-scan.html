Why — The Decision Ledger

8 weeks · UX Designer (team of 2)
Relay · Feature Design

Problem

Teams make decisions continuously. The reasoning disappears into chat, files, and email — invisible to anyone who wasn't there.

Insight

The problem isn't retrieval — it's capture. Every existing method asks too much at the wrong moment, so capture fails entirely.

Solution

Two-mode decision ledger: Quick Capture (3 fields, under 60s) + Full Entry — both in a searchable, structured record.

Problem Space

A missing record of reasoning is invisible. And invisible problems don't get fixed.

Decisions scatter across chat, design files, email replies, and meeting notes. Each captures a piece. None make context retrievable later — and the people who need it most pay the cost every time.

New Joiner

Needs to reconstruct "why" before contributing. Ramp-up longer than necessary.

Mid-Project Inheritor

Inherits output of decisions — not the reasoning. Wrong assumptions become rework.

The Manager

Becomes single source of institutional memory. Interrupted constantly. Pattern repeats.

Key insight

The cost of missing context is invisible — you don't know you're working from wrong assumptions until something breaks.

Research finding

4 tools audited. None capture a decision as a structured artifact at the moment it happens and make it retrievable centrally later.

How Might We

"Make capturing a decision easier than not capturing it?"

Ideation

We explored 3 directions that tested opposite ends of the same tension.

Rather than variations of one idea, we designed from different assumptions — then compared where each broke down. Direction 1 broke on motivation. Direction 2 broke on orientation. Direction 3 resolved both.

01 · Single Form

All fields upfront

Broke on: motivation. 9 fields = homework.

Kept: consistent, queryable output.

02 · Progressive

One field at a time

Broke on: orientation. "Form that keeps going."

Kept: zero barrier to starting.

Why showing discarded directions matters

Directions 1 and 2 weren't wasted effort — they sharpened why Direction 3 worked. Showing what you considered and discarded (and why) is stronger evidence of design thinking than showing only the chosen direction.

Design Decisions

Every decision had a reason. Here are the ones that mattered most.

01

No labels on Quick Capture fields

Removed labels after testing — they increased perceived complexity with no comprehension benefit. Placeholder text guides input. 3/5 participants said the form felt "like a lot" with labels; 0/5 had confusion without them.

Evidence

3/5 testers: "a lot" with labels. 0/5 confused without them.

02

"Quick Log" marker on partial records

Risk: the marker devalues partial records. Resolved in testing — it sets expectation rather than judgment. Users trusted the ledger more when entries were labeled clearly, not less. A partial record that exists beats a complete record never written.

Tension navigated

Devalued vs. trusted. Testing resolved toward labeled = useful signal.

03

AI suggests rationale — user edits, no auto-save

We explicitly rejected AI auto-documenting decisions. If the ledger reads as AI-generated, people stop trusting it. The boundary: AI reduces the blank-page problem. The user's words shape the final record.

What we rejected

Auto-documented decisions. AI ownership of the record kills trust.

Scope

What we deliberately didn't build — and why it was the right call.

Scope decisions are design decisions. Naming what was left out makes the current version's priorities legible — and signals that trade-offs were considered, not overlooked.

Free-form rich content in entries

Images, diagrams, narrative blocks inside entries — like Notion. Scoped out because the guided structure is the core value proposition. Free-form is a v2 addition once capture behavior is established.

Natural v2 extension

Direct Figma frame linking

Participants mentioned linking to specific Figma frames. Deprioritized because it requires integration work disproportionate to v1 core — but a natural extension of linked references already in the design.

Reflection

What I'd do differently — and what this clarified.

What held up

Test your own design.

The first version of the capture form — we wouldn't have used it ourselves. That question reshaped everything. The fastest and most honest test available.

Design for motivation, not discipline.

Users under workload choose the easier path every time. Making the right action feel like the easiest action is the actual design work.

Start from retrieval.

Working backwards from "what does someone need to find 6 months from now?" separated essential from optional at capture time.

★ What I'd do differently

01 — Test the trigger, not just the form

We tested form flows but not the moment someone decides to open Quick Capture. That's the real failure point. Next time I'd prototype the trigger interaction first.

02 — Test with engineers under deadline

4/5 participants were designers or PMs. The hardest adoption case — engineers mid-sprint — wasn't tested. That's the gap I'd close in a v2 research plan.

03 — Longitudinal validation

Usability testing shows learnability, not sustained adoption. Did teams maintain the ledger after 4 weeks? That's the question current data doesn't answer.
