Design Process

8 weeks — how the project unfolded

A vague brief → a specific constraint → a two-mode solution.

01

Discover — What's actually broken?

Weeks 1–2

Starting point: a brief to "improve knowledge sharing for remote teams." We needed to understand the specific failure mode before designing anything.

4 tools audited 3 persona types mapped Consistent gap: no capture-as-structured-artifact
02

Reframe — From "knowledge sharing" to motivation design

Week 3

The reframe that changed everything

The brief said "make decisions easier to find." The real problem was: the capture never happens in the first place. We were designing a retrieval solution for a capture problem.

Original brief

"Improve knowledge sharing for remote teams."

Too broad. No actionable constraint.

Reframed to

"Why does capture fail at the moment of decision — and how do we remove that barrier?"

Constraint: motivation at capture time, not technology.

How Might We →

"How might we make capturing a decision easier than not capturing it?"

04

Decide — Key design decisions with evidence

Weeks 5–7
D1

No labels on Quick Capture fields

Evidence: Testing showed labels increased perceived complexity with no comprehension benefit. 3/5 participants said the form felt "like a lot" with labels. 0/5 had confusion without them.

D2

"Quick Log" marker on partial records

Tension navigated: Would marking partial records devalue them? Testing resolved it — the marker set expectation rather than judgment. Users trusted the ledger more, not less, when entries were labeled clearly.

D3

AI suggests rationale — user edits, no auto-save

What we explicitly rejected: AI auto-documenting decisions. If the ledger reads as AI-generated, people stop trusting it. The boundary: AI reduces the blank-page problem. The user's words shape the final record.

05

Validate — What held, what we'd change

Week 8

Held up in testing

  • →5/5 found Quick Capture without instruction
  • →No participant chose Full Entry for low-stakes decisions
  • →"Quick Log" marker improved ledger trust, not lowered it

Would change next time

  • →Test the trigger moment, not just the form
  • →Include engineers under deadline in user pool
  • →Longitudinal study: 4-week real-use adoption
Design Challenge

The tension we had to resolve — and how we resolved it

Capture must be

  • –Under 60 seconds
  • –No context switch required
  • –Low cognitive load
  • –Feels like sending a message

vs.

Retrieval must be

  • –Rich enough to be useful 6 months later
  • –Structured and searchable
  • –What, why, and alternatives
  • –Understandable without the author

How we resolved it

Two-mode design: Quick Capture satisfies the capture side. Full Entry satisfies the retrieval side. Both land in the same searchable ledger — the user chooses based on their moment, not the system's requirement. Neither mode penalizes. A partial record that exists beats a complete record never written.

Reflection

What this project clarified about the design process

Test your own design.

We built the first version, stepped back, and asked whether we'd actually use it at the moment it mattered. The answer was no. That question reshaped everything that followed.

Options explored and discarded are part of the story.

Directions 1 and 2 were not wasted effort. They sharpened our understanding of why Direction 3 worked. The tradeoffs made the final decision legible — to us, to the team, and to the stakeholders we presented to.

Let retrieval drive capture.

Starting from "what does someone need to find in the ledger six months from now?" and working backwards made it easier to identify what was essential at capture time — and what was optional.
