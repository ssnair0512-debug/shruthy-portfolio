Overview

Relay is a workplace async communication platform built for teams operating across time zones, schedules, and working patterns. It brings messaging, email, scheduling, and team coordination into one place — reducing the friction that comes from switching between disconnected tools.

Why is a feature inside Relay. It is the decision ledger — a dedicated space where teams can log, organize, and retrieve the reasoning behind decisions, so that context does not get lost as work moves between people, projects, and phases.

Problem Space

Information lives everywhere. None of it is findable when it actually matters.

Decisions scatter across many surfaces at once — a message in chat, a comment in a design file, an email reply, a note from a meeting. Each captures a piece. None of them make context retrievable later.

The New Joiner

Gets partial answers. Needs to reconstruct "why" before they can contribute. The ramp-up is longer than it needs to be.

The Inheritor

Inherits the output of past decisions — not the reasoning behind them. Getting it wrong shows up in rework.

The Manager

Holds more context than anyone. Gets messaged constantly to provide it. The pattern repeats indefinitely.

The root cause

A missing record of reasoning is invisible. Because it is invisible, it rarely gets identified or fixed. Time spent reconstructing known context is time not spent moving forward — and the cost compounds with every handoff.

Research

What the Research Pointed To

We looked at the tools teams already use to manage this problem. Understanding where existing solutions fall short was essential before designing anything.

④ Inline callout — replaces side column box. Full reading width kept.

Why we did this first

Before designing anything new, we needed to understand precisely where existing tools break down. Each category solves part of the problem — knowing which part lets us design around the gap, not into it.

Messaging platforms — context buried
Context buried

Messaging Platforms Teams, Slack

Built around conversation, not decisions. A decision buried inside a 40-message thread is technically present — but you can search for keywords, not for structured reasoning.

Documentation platforms — friction at capture
Friction at capture

Documentation Platforms Confluence, Notion

Right format, wrong timing. Creating a document means switching tools after the decision — when energy and context have already passed. Under real workload, it doesn't happen consistently.

Video tools — not queryable
Not queryable

Video Tools Loom

Excellent for explaining complexity. But a video can't be queried across a library for a specific piece of reasoning — it's a communication format, not a retrieval system.

Task management — what, never why
What, never why

Task Management Tools Jira, Asana

Tracks what is being done and whether it is done. The reasoning behind decisions — how work was scoped — is absent by design.

Consistent gap

No existing tool is designed to capture a decision as a structured artifact at the moment it happens and make it retrievable from a central place later.

Design Challenge

How do you make capturing a decision easier than not capturing it?

Capture side — must be

  • –Instant — under 60 seconds
  • –No context switch required
  • –Works between meetings
  • –Feels as light as sending a message

Retrieval side — must be

  • –Rich enough to be useful 6 months later
  • –Structured and searchable
  • –Includes what, why, and alternatives
  • –Understandable without the author present

"Any solution that requires significant effort at the point of capture will not be used consistently. That is not a discipline problem. It is a design problem."

The people who most need to fill it out are the people with the least motivation to do so in the moment. Documenting it feels like administrative work for someone else's future benefit.

Ideation

What we explored before landing on a direction

We worked backwards from retrieval: what is the minimum someone needs to provide for a decision record to be genuinely useful to someone else six months later? That question separated essential from nice-to-have — and showed us that not every decision needs the same documentation depth.

Direction 01

Single Structured Form

One form, all fields visible upfront. Fill it completely or not at all.

Why we moved on

9 fields at once feels like homework. Easy to close, rarely returned to.

Direction 02

Progressive Disclosure

Start with a single field. Reveal more as the user continues.

Why we moved on

Disorienting — users unsure if done. "The form that keeps going."

★ Chosen
Quick

Direction 03 — Chosen

Two Modes — Quick & Full

Quick Capture: 3 fields, under a minute. Full Entry: complete structured record. Same ledger destination regardless of mode.

Why this direction worked

Respects where the user actually is. Neither mode penalizes. A partial record that exists is more valuable than a complete record never written.

Design Decisions

How each piece of the experience was built

The Quick Capture Flow

Designed to feel like sending a message

We designed Quick Capture to feel close to sending a message — familiar, low-commitment, fast. The three fields are stacked simply. No labels cluttering the view. Placeholder text does the work of guiding input. A single tag selector lets the entry land in the right area of the ledger without requiring the user to think hard about categorization.

④ "Key decision" inline — was in 240px side column, now readable in main flow

Key decision

Quick Capture can expand to Full Entry at any point — one tap, no loss of what was already typed. We did not force users to choose upfront; we let them start small and add more if the moment called for it.

The entry is saved with a "Quick Log" marker, which signals to future readers that this record may have less depth — but it is still searchable and still there. A partial record that exists is more valuable than a complete record that was never written.

Quick Capture UI — Three-Field Entry Flow
The Full Entry Flow

Structured but not overwhelming

Full Entry is designed for decisions that carry more weight. Fields are grouped into two sections: the core record — what was decided, why, what else was considered — and the context layer — who was involved, what it links to, when to revisit.

Key decision

The context layer is collapsible. Filling only the core record produces a complete, useful entry. The collapsible section is an invitation, not a requirement.

We also built in support for linked references — so a decision entry can connect to a Figma file, a document, or a specific message thread. This addresses one of the real pain points from research: the context that surrounds a decision is often as important as the decision itself.

On rich media

Full entries support embedded video for additional context. A Loom inside a structured decision entry is a different story from a standalone video — it becomes context that lives alongside searchable information rather than a floating artifact in a folder.

Full Entry UI — Core Record + Collapsible Context Layer
AI Assistance in the Capture Flow

Reducing the blank-page problem

Relay has AI capabilities that surface inside the Why feature in a specific way. When a decision is being logged, the AI can suggest a rationale draft based on recent relevant activity in the workspace — messages, threads, or meeting notes connected to the topic. The user reviews and edits the draft. Nothing is auto-saved without confirmation.

What AI does — and doesn't do

It is not AI doing the documentation for the user. It is AI reducing the blank-page problem. Starting from a suggested draft that is 60 percent right is meaningfully easier than starting from nothing. The user's judgment and the user's words still shape the final record.

The Ledger View

Built for retrieval, not just storage

Entries in the ledger are browseable and searchable. The search experience is designed around the question someone actually asks: "why did we decide to do X?" Not keyword matching across full text, but structured retrieval by decision area, tag, stakeholder, and date range.

Search design logic

Designed around the question someone actually asks: "why did we decide to do X?" — not keyword matching across full text, but structured retrieval by decision area, tag, stakeholder, and date range. Full-text search is a secondary layer.

The ledger surfaces recent entries first, with filters for area, contributor, and date. Each entry shows a preview — the title and the decision — without requiring the user to open it to understand what it contains.

Scope

What We Did Not Build (Yet)

Why naming this matters

Scope decisions are design decisions. Naming what was deliberately left out — and why — makes the current version's priorities legible.

A few ideas that surfaced during the project are worth naming because they shaped our understanding of where this feature could go — even if they are outside the scope of this phase.

Free-form documentation within entries

The current structure is intentionally guided. A future iteration could include a free-form section alongside the structured fields, giving contributors space to add screenshots, diagrams, or narrative context. This would make entries more complete without changing the capture experience.

Connections to design tools

Several team members mentioned how useful it would be to link directly to specific Figma frames alongside a decision. This is a natural extension of the linked references feature.

Reflection

What this project clarified about the design process

Test your own design.

The most important insight from this project came from testing the design on ourselves. We built the first version of the capture form, stepped back, and asked whether we would actually use it at the moment it mattered. The answer was no. That question reshaped everything that followed.

Design for motivation, not discipline.

It is easy to design a tool that assumes users are organised and have time. The harder and more honest design challenge is building for users who are busy, slightly distracted, and who will choose the easier path every time one is available. Making the right action feel like the easiest action is the actual work.

Options explored and discarded are part of the story.

Direction 1 and Direction 2 were not wasted effort. They sharpened our understanding of why Direction 3 worked. The tradeoffs made those decisions legible — to us, to the team, and to the stakeholders we presented to.

Let the retrieval experience drive the capture experience.

We started by understanding what someone needs to find in the ledger six months from now, and then worked backwards to understand what has to go in at capture time for that retrieval to be possible. Starting from retrieval made it much easier to identify what was essential and what was optional.
