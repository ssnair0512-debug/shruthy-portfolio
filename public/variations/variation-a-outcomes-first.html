Problem Space

Information lives everywhere. None of it is findable when it actually matters.

Decisions scatter across chat threads, design files, email replies, and meeting notes. Each captures a piece. None make context retrievable later — and the people who need it most (new joiners, project inheritors) pay the cost every time.

The New Joiner

Gets partial answers. Needs to reconstruct "why" before contributing. Ramp-up longer than it needs to be.

The Inheritor

Inherits output of past decisions — not the reasoning. Wrong assumptions show up as rework.

The Manager

Holds all context. Gets interrupted constantly to provide it. The pattern repeats.

How Might We

"How might we make capturing a decision easier than not capturing it — so that context survives handoffs, onboarding, and time?"

Why this framing: The problem isn't that people don't know they should document decisions. It's that every existing method asks too much at the wrong moment. The constraint we designed from was motivation, not information architecture.

Limitation we'd address next

Testing was conducted with 5 participants on prototype flows — not in-product with real data. A longitudinal study (did teams maintain the ledger after 4+ weeks?) is the validation we don't have yet. That's what I'd prioritize before a v2 build.

Ideation — 3 Directions Explored

What we explored before landing on a direction

We worked backwards from retrieval: what's the minimum someone needs to provide for a decision record to be genuinely useful six months later? That question separated essential from nice-to-have.

Direction 01

Single Structured Form

9 fields at once feels like homework. Easy to close, rarely returned to.

Kept: consistent, queryable output structure.

Direction 02

Progressive Disclosure

"The form that keeps going." Users felt disoriented — unsure when they were done.

Kept: zero barrier to starting.

Direction 03 — Chosen

Two Modes: Quick & Full

Respects where the user is. Both modes land in the same searchable ledger. No penalty for choosing either.

What I'd do differently

01

Test the trigger moment, not just the form. We tested the form flows but not whether someone actually opens Quick Capture in the first place. That's the real failure point — and I'd prototype it first next time.

02

Test with engineers under deadline. 4 of 5 participants were designers or PMs — already disposed to structured documentation. Engineers mid-sprint are the hardest adoption case, and we didn't test that group.

03

Run a longitudinal study. Usability testing shows learnability. It doesn't show whether teams maintain the ledger after 4 weeks of real use — and that's the question the current data doesn't answer.

Test your own design.

We built the first version of the capture form, stepped back, and asked whether we'd actually use it. The answer was no. That question reshaped everything that followed.

Design for motivation, not discipline.

The harder and more honest challenge is building for users who are busy and will choose the easier path every time. Making the right action feel like the easiest action is the actual work.

Let retrieval drive capture.

Starting from "what does someone need to find in the ledger six months from now?" made it much easier to identify what was essential at capture time — and what was optional.
