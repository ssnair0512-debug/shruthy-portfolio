WYD — Social Event Planning

I was asked to design an events app.
What I actually designed was a way for people to show up for each other.

Act 1 — The Problem

Planning a hangout with friends used to take four apps

Instagram to find out what's happening. iMessage to coordinate with the group. Google Calendar to block the time. Snapchat to check if anyone was actually coming. Four apps, twenty minutes of context-switching, and you still weren't sure if people were going to show up.

The brief was deliberately open: "design an app for social event planning." I spent the first week trying to understand if this was actually a problem worth solving — or if the existing apps were good enough and people just had different preferences.

Turns out it was a real problem. And it was more specific than the brief implied.

Act 2 — What I Found

The research changed what I thought the problem was

I surveyed 48 people and ran 10 moderated usability tests with existing event tools (Instagram Events, Facebook Events, iMessage group chats). I had hypotheses going in. Most of them were wrong, in instructive ways.

"There's no single place where I can see what my friends are doing this weekend. I have to check everywhere and still feel like I'm missing things."

— Survey respondent, 23
65%

couldn't easily see what friends were doing on any given weekend — even though everyone was posting constantly

70%

had missed events because details were scattered — someone texted in one thread, posted on another, and the information never made it into one place

80%

found creating events on existing apps too complicated or too public — the cost of posting felt higher than the value of coordination

Key Insight

The problem wasn't discovery — it was coordination overhead. People knew how to find events. They couldn't coordinate around them without the process feeling like work.

Act 3 — Reframing the Question

From "events app" to "coordination layer"

The original brief suggested I was designing an events discovery tool. The research suggested I was actually designing infrastructure for how small groups of close friends make plans.

That distinction changed everything about the architecture. Discovery-first apps (Eventbrite, Meetup, Facebook Events) optimize for breadth — helping you find things to do. What my users needed was depth — a place where their specific friend group could see each other's availability, coordinate around plans, and keep the social record of what they'd done together.

How might we design a single, intuitive platform that makes planning and discovering social events with close friends feel effortless — without the coordination overhead that currently makes people give up?

— Reframed problem statement

Act 4 — Design Decisions

Three choices I made and why I made them

1

Private circles, not public feeds

I scoped the app to close friend groups, not open event discovery. This wasn't a compromise — it was the actual design insight. When the audience is your friends, posting feels natural. When it's the internet, it feels performative.

2

One-screen event creation

The existing flow required 4 steps: title, date/time, location, invite. 40% of users quit at the invite step during testing. I collapsed it into a single progressive form with smart defaults. You can create an event in under 30 seconds.

3

Availability as the home surface

Instead of a browse-and-discover feed, the home screen shows your friends' upcoming plans. You open the app and immediately see what's happening with your people — not a curated list of events from strangers.

Result

What changed

I tested the redesigned create event flow against the original 4-step flow with the same usability test participants. The delta was significant enough to validate the core design decision.

Before

60%

completion rate — 4-step create event flow, existing pattern

After

91%

completion rate — redesigned single-screen progressive flow

Beyond the headline metric: users reported the app replaced their use of 4 separate apps for social coordination. The "4 apps → 1" consolidation was the core promise of the reframe — and it held up in testing.

Honest Reflection

What I got wrong and what I'd do differently

I built too much before testing the core concept

I had mid-fidelity wireframes of 7 screens before I tested whether the core model (availability-first home feed) resonated. I should have tested a concept sketch of just the home surface before building anything around it.

Next time → Paper prototype the core thesis in week 2, not week 6.

The "during event" section was scope creep I let happen

The journey map surfaced opportunities across before/during/after. I designed features for all three phases. The "during event" features (live map, shared itinerary) were interesting but not core to the problem statement I'd defined. I spent a week on them.

Next time → Define a clear scope boundary before the journey map exercise, not after.

My sample was too homogeneous

48 survey respondents were mostly 20–26, primarily iOS users, primarily urban. The coordination patterns for different demographics (families, older adults) could look very different — and I didn't test those assumptions.

Next time → Define minimum diversity criteria for the survey sample before launching it.
