Case Study

WUD! — Reimagining the Social Event Lifecycle

Role

Product Designer — Research, Journey Mapping, Concept Ideation, Prototyping, High-Fidelity Design

Timeline

July – December 2025

Tools

Figma, Adobe Creative Suite

I went in expecting to design a better search experience.
I came out having designed a system for how people show up — and show up together — in their social lives.

Event planning is social. The tools people use to plan events aren't. Users were already juggling Eventbrite for discovery, WhatsApp for coordination, Google Calendar for tracking, and Instagram for social proof — and none of these talked to each other. WUD! was an opportunity to design the connective tissue that was missing.

Context

The Brief I Was Given — and the One I Chose to Answer

Design an intuitive event app that simplifies discovery, planning, and participation. That brief had an assumption baked in: that discovery was the problem. Before designing anything, I wanted to know if that was actually true.

The brief also didn't specify whether "planning" meant the host's experience or the attendee's — a distinction that turned out to be central to the design. Resolving that ambiguity was the first design decision I made.

This was a solo project — I owned every stage from research design through final delivery. The reframe from discovery to coordination and identity was my call, made after the research challenged my first assumption.

Starting assumption

The biggest barrier was discoverability. Research confirmed this was only partially true.

Research

Navigating the "Obvious" Problem

My initial assumption was that the biggest barrier was discoverability. I designed a mixed-methods study to challenge it — because designing confidently for the wrong problem at full fidelity is expensive.

n = 10 01 · Observation Study

Watching without prompting

Shadowed 10 users in natural navigation contexts. What they did without being asked revealed something consistent: they opened other apps to coordinate mid-browse, and they did it without noticing.

7 platforms 02 · Competitor Audit

Where the market stopped short

Eventbrite and Meetup had solved centralized discovery reasonably well. Everything that happens after finding an event — coordination, social proof, post-event identity — was failing across all 7 platforms.

n = 5 03 · Qualitative Interviews

The quote that changed everything

Five in-depth sessions focused on coordination frustrations. One participant — age 24 — said something that shifted the entire direction of the project.

n = 48 04 · Quantitative Survey

Separating personal from systemic

Validated interview findings at scale. Confirmed the coordination and identity gaps weren't just individual frustrations — they were systemic patterns across the user base.

Findings

What the Data Actually Said

"I know there are cool things happening — I just never know if my friends are going, so I don't bother." — Interview participant, age 24

That quote was the pivot. It wasn't about not finding events. It was about the social risk of going alone — and having no tool to reduce it.

65%

wanted better personalisation — felt buried under irrelevant suggestions

70%

found coordinating with friends difficult; regularly forced to WhatsApp off-platform

80%

wanted to showcase event history socially — a need no platform was addressing

The 80% identity finding was the biggest surprise. Users weren't just trying to attend events. They were trying to be seen as people who do interesting things. No existing platform was designing for that need. That gap became the core of the brief I chose to answer.

Reframe

Reframing the Problem

The original brief asked for "better event discovery." After the research, that wasn't the right target.

How Might We

"How might we reduce the friction of group coordination and social validation in event planning, so that users never need to leave the app to do the social work that makes events actually happen?"

This reframe shifted the design target from solo discovery to group participation. From finding events to making attendance feel socially safe and practically frictionless. The three design pillars — personalised discovery, in-app coordination, and social identity — came directly from this reframe.

Design

The Three Principles I Designed Against

Before touching screens, I defined three principles grounded directly in the research — and one structural constraint that shaped the entire IA: the same user could be both attendee and host simultaneously. The design had to support both modes within a single account.

1

Keep the group together.

Any coordination that happens off-platform is a product failure. The app needs to make group planning feel less chaotic than a group chat.

2

Earn relevance, don't assume it.

Surfacing irrelevant events erodes trust faster than having no recommendations at all. Personalization must feel intentional — collected upfront, not inferred silently.

3

Let users build an identity.

Social proof isn't vanity — it's how people decide who to trust and what to try. The profile isn't an afterthought; it's a social signal and a record of lived experience.

Grounding filter

Every design decision was tested against one question: does this reduce the need to go off-platform?

Dual persona constraint

Same account, two modes — Attendee and Host. This structural decision shaped the entire information architecture.

Process

What Failed, and What Changed

Two prototype rounds, 10 users each. Three core tasks: discover an event, coordinate attendance with friends, create and manage as a host. Here's where the real design work happened.

RSVP status — visually clean, functionally ambiguous

The initial RSVP tracking UI used subtle status indicators that 4 of 10 users regularly misread. The design looked right. It wasn't readable. Users couldn't tell if a friend had confirmed or was still pending without careful reading.

→
What changed

Redesigned with distinct color, iconography, and explicit label — status readable at a glance, not deduced.

Animation — the UI competing with itself

The first pass at animations was too aggressive. Users described the interface as "busy" and "distracting." The UI was competing with the social content it was meant to surface.

→
The rule I landed on

Motion should confirm an action, not announce it. Pulled back animation intensity significantly across the board.

Create Event — 14 fields, one scroll, 60% completion

The creation form covered everything from image upload through cancellation policy on a single screen. Hosts lost orientation mid-form and abandoned before completing. The cognitive load was too high.

→
What changed

Split into a 4-step progressive disclosure flow. Each step groups related decisions: basic info → social settings → RSVP configuration → policies. Step completion rate improved from 60% to 91% in retesting.

Solution

The Three Problems, Solved

Problem 01

Clutter and Irrelevant Discovery

Profile Preferences lets users define their interests during onboarding — so every recommendation is earned rather than guessed. Explore surfaces two discovery paths: social invitations (warm) above neighbourhood events (cold), prioritizing social context over algorithmic suggestion. Map and Calendar give additional lenses: spatial and time-based.

Key feature

Interests collected upfront — event types, travel distance, preferred time of day. Every recommendation is earned, not inferred.

Note

The filter panel covers 5 dimensions — Distance, Date, Category, Price, and Social — each mapping to a gap identified in the competitor audit. Existing platforms offered at most 2 of these.

Problem 02

The Coordination Gap

Event Details surfaces network data directly: attendee avatars, follow-status badges, and tickets remaining — answering "is anyone I know going?" without requiring a group chat. The RSVP flow is three steps and ends with a clear confirmation state that signals the social coordination work is done. Hosts get checkbox-based bulk removal for attendee lists — tested significantly better than swipe-to-delete for lists over 10 people.

Key insight

Surfacing network context on the event detail page itself eliminated the primary reason users abandoned events mid-planning.

Problem 03

Identity and Social Validation

Profile displays Following, Followers, and Events Attended as equal social stats — treating participation as social currency on par with relationships. My Events uses three tabs: Event Tickets (QR codes), Saved Events, and Hosted Events with inline editing. The Following list appends event context to each connection — turning it into a live feed of what people you know are doing.

Key insight

80% wanted social event history. No existing platform was designing for this need. The profile becomes a record of lived experience.

Reflection

What I'd Do Differently

There are three things I'd change if I ran this project again:

Go deeper on group consensus dynamics

The coordination features I designed addressed the problem at the interaction level. Looking back, the hardest part of planning an event with friends isn't tracking RSVPs — it's reaching consensus on what to attend in the first place. A vote-on-options or shared shortlisting tool would have targeted that friction more directly.

Next time

Run a dedicated co-design session around group decision-making before defining the coordination feature set.

Instrument the prototype earlier

Usability testing captured task completion, but I didn't have metrics on where users slowed down within a flow. That behavioral data would have surfaced micro-friction before the final test round.

Next time

Even simple time-on-task logging per step would have identified bottlenecks in round one — not round two.

Give the host experience equal research weight

Most of my interview participants were event attendees. With more host-side research, the Create Event flow might have been structured differently — particularly visibility and capacity settings, which hosts found most difficult in testing. The host experience was underserved, and it shows in places.

Next time

Minimum 3 host participants per research round. Test host flows with equal rigor to attendee flows from round one.

Limitation

Host-side experience deprioritized — designed late and tested in only one round.

If I ran this again

Instrument earlier, go deeper on group consensus dynamics, and test host flows with equal rigor to attendee flows.

Takeaway

The Bigger Lesson

This project taught me that functional problems and emotional problems require different design responses.

The functional problem — finding events — was addressable with better filters and a cleaner UI. The emotional problem — coordination anxiety and the desire to be seen as someone with an interesting life — required features that felt less like tools and more like social infrastructure.

The pivot happened because the research challenged my first assumption. I went in expecting to design a better search experience. I came out having designed a system for how people show up — and show up together — in their social lives.

The journey map was the turning point. Mapping emotion across Before/During/After made the scope of the problem undeniable — the design opportunity wasn't in the app experience itself, it was in everything that happened at the edges of it.
Core lesson

Functional problems and emotional problems require different design responses. Don't mistake one for the other.
