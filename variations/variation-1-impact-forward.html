Context

The brief was vague on purpose

The prompt: design an app for social event planning. No target user. No defined scope. No constraints. I had to decide what "social event planning" even meant before I could design anything.

I started by questioning the premise. Who is planning what, with whom, and where is that process breaking down today? Those questions shaped every research decision that followed.

Research

Finding the real problem

I ran a survey with 48 respondents and conducted 10 moderated usability tests to map how people currently plan social events — what tools they used, where things fell apart, and what they actually needed.

65%

struggled to see what their friends were doing on any given weekend

70%

had missed events because information was scattered across apps

80%

found creating events on existing platforms too complicated or too public

The pattern was consistent: people weren't failing to plan because they didn't care. They were failing because the tools made coordination harder than it needed to be. Instagram for discovery. iMessage for coordination. Google Calendar for logistics. Snapchat for attendance checks. Four apps. One hangout.

Problem Framing

Reframing the question

The original brief pointed at "event planning." But the research showed something more specific:

How might we

Design a single, intuitive platform that makes planning and discovering social events with close friends feel effortless — without the context-switching that currently kills momentum?

This reframe was load-bearing. It shifted the scope from "events app" to "coordination layer" — which changed every information architecture decision that followed.

Key Design Decisions

Three choices that defined the outcome

Decision 01

Single audience: close friends, not the public

80% of users said they didn't want their events visible to their full social network. I scoped the app to a private friend group model — not a discovery feed for strangers. This cut scope significantly and made the coordination layer coherent.

Decision 02

Collapsing event creation to one flow

Usability tests showed the 4-step create event flow had a 60% completion rate. Users quit at step 3 (inviting friends) because the input felt like work. I redesigned it as a single progressive form with smart defaults, reducing cognitive load at each step.

Decision 03

Availability over discovery

Users didn't want to browse events like a marketplace. They wanted to know if their friends were free this weekend. I prioritized a "what's happening with your people" feed over a public events catalog.

Solution

What shipped

WYD consolidates discovery, coordination, and logistics into one surface. The home feed shows what friends have coming up. Event creation is a single screen. RSVP and coordination live in the same thread as the event.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

I validated the solution too late

I built mid-fidelity prototypes before getting feedback on core flows. An earlier round of concept testing would have caught the notification model problems before I'd designed around them.

The journey map was thorough but led me astray briefly

Mapping the full event lifecycle (before / during / after) was useful for framing, but I spent time designing "during event" features that fell outside the core thesis. Tighter scope definition earlier would have saved a week.

I'd test on Android earlier

My usability tests were iOS-only. The mental model differences for navigation patterns are real, and I didn't account for them until late in the process.
