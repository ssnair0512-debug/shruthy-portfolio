Problem Framing

Breaking down a vague brief

The prompt was deliberately open: "design a social event planning app." No defined user. No constraints. I treated this as the actual design challenge — understanding the space before choosing a direction was the real first task.

I identified three sub-questions embedded in the brief: Who is doing the planning? What's actually breaking down in their current process? And is this a discovery problem, a coordination problem, or both?

Research answered these questions. The reframe followed.

How Might We

Design a single, intuitive platform that makes planning and discovering social events with close friends feel effortless — without the coordination overhead that currently makes people give up?

Research

How I built the evidence base

Screener Survey

n = 48

Quantified the scope of the problem: frequency of social planning, apps used, points of failure, and willingness to switch to a new tool.

Moderated Usability Tests

n = 10

Observed participants creating and coordinating around events using their current tools (Instagram Events, iMessage, Facebook Events) to identify specific friction points.

I chose a mixed-methods approach because the survey could tell me how widespread the problem was, but not why it was happening. The usability tests on existing tools surfaced the specific moments where coordination broke down — which the survey alone couldn't have shown.

Findings

What the data showed

65%

couldn't easily see what their friends were doing on a given weekend despite everyone being active on social media

70%

had missed events because information was scattered — text here, post there, no single source of truth

80%

found creating events on existing apps too complicated or too public — the friction cost outweighed the coordination benefit

Research → Design

How evidence drove decisions

Decision chain 01

Why I scoped to close friends, not public events

Evidence

80% said existing apps felt "too public" — they didn't want their full network to see every plan

→

Insight

The problem isn't feature gaps — it's audience mismatch. Close friends ≠ social network.

→

Decision

Private circles model only. No public feed. No discovery from strangers.

Decision chain 02

Why I collapsed event creation to a single screen

Evidence

60% completion rate on 4-step create event flow — users dropped off at the invite step

→

Insight

Each additional step multiplies abandonment. Inviting people is the hardest step, so it should come last with minimum friction.

→

Decision

Single progressive form. Smart defaults for all fields. Invite step is optional at creation time.

Decision chain 03

Why the home surface shows availability, not events

Evidence

65% said they can't tell what their friends are doing this weekend, even with everyone on social media

→

Insight

The bottleneck is legibility of friends' plans, not event discovery. Users want a status check, not a catalog.

→

Decision

Home feed = friends' upcoming plans. Not a browse-for-events surface.

Design Decisions

Options considered and choices made

Information architecture: event-centric vs. friend-centric

Not chosen

Event-centric — browse events, filter by category, RSVP

Chosen

Friend-centric — see what your people are doing, join or create plans around them

Event-centric mirrors Eventbrite and Facebook Events, which users were already familiar with and not using for close-friend coordination. Friend-centric matched the actual mental model: "What is Sarah doing this weekend?" not "What events are happening near me?"

Create event: wizard flow vs. single form

Not chosen

4-step wizard: Title → Date → Location → Invite

Chosen

Single progressive form with optional fields and smart defaults

Wizard flows feel thorough but demand commitment at each step. The progressive form lets users create a skeleton event quickly (title + time = 15 seconds) and fill in details later. This matched how people actually plan: loosely first, precisely later.

Honest Reflection

Trade-offs, limitations, and what I'd do differently

Scope Decision

I designed features for before/during/after the event lifecycle. The "during event" features (shared itinerary, live location) were interesting but not core to the reframed problem. I spent a week building screens I shouldn't have prioritized.

Next time: Define scope boundaries before the journey map, not after.

Testing Timing

I built mid-fidelity wireframes of 7 screens before testing the core model (availability-first home feed). An earlier, lower-fidelity test would have validated the thesis before I built around it.

Next time: Concept test the core model by week 2, not the prototype by week 7.

Sample Homogeneity

48 survey respondents skewed 20–26, iOS-only, urban. The coordination patterns for other demographics could look very different. The findings are valid for the target user segment but not generalizable.

Next time: Set minimum diversity criteria for the sample before launching any survey.

What's Missing

I didn't design for edge cases: what happens when someone creates a plan and nobody RSVPs? How does the notification model work without becoming noise? These weren't surfaced until late in usability testing.

Next time: Map failure states and edge cases before finalizing any flow.
