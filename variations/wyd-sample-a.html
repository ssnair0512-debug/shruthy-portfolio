Overview

Event planning is social. The tools people use to plan events aren't.

4 → 1
apps consolidated into a single lifecycle
3
core friction points designed out of the experience

Users were already juggling Eventbrite for discovery, WhatsApp for coordination, Google Calendar for tracking, and Instagram for social proof — and none of these talked to each other. WUD! was an opportunity to design the connective tissue that was missing: a single app that covers the full social event lifecycle from discovery to memory.

I owned this project end to end — from research scoping through final prototype. I worked alone.

Research

Navigating the "Obvious" Problem

Starting Assumption

The biggest barrier was discoverability — users couldn't find relevant events. This turned out to be only partially true.

To challenge that assumption, I adopted a mixed-methods research approach designed to capture both what users were doing and why they felt frustrated. Observing 10 users in natural contexts — without prompting — revealed the gap between stated preferences and actual behavior.

01

Observation Study

Shadowed 10 users (students and professionals) tracking natural navigation without prompting. Seeing behavior in context revealed where real drop-off points were.

02

Competitor Audit

Analyzed 7 platforms including Eventbrite and Meetup — what the market solved well (centralized discovery) and where it failed (everything after you find an event).

03

Qualitative Interviews

5 in-depth sessions focused on coordination frustrations — surfacing the emotional dimension of the problem, the anxiety, not just the inconvenience.

04

Quantitative Survey

48 respondents used to separate personal frustrations from systemic patterns across different user types.

What the Data Actually Said

"I know there are cool things happening — I just never know if my friends are going, so I don't bother."
— Interview participant, age 24
65%
explicitly requested better personalisation — they felt buried under irrelevant suggestions.
70%
found coordinating events with friends difficult and were regularly forced off-platform to WhatsApp.
80%
wanted a way to showcase their event history socially — a need no existing platform addressed.

The user journey map was the tool that turned scattered interview data into a design agenda. It plotted emotion over time across three phases — Before, During, After — and revealed that pain clustered around coordination before the event and identity after it. The event itself was largely positive. This meant the product didn't need to compete with the event — just support the edges.

Key Insight

The discovery problem was real, but solvable. The coordination problem was where the experience truly broke down — and was being almost completely ignored by competitors. The 80% identity statistic was the most surprising: users weren't just trying to attend events, they were trying to be seen as people who do interesting things.

Reframe

From "Better Discovery" to "Social Infrastructure"

The original brief asked for a better event discovery experience. The research changed the target entirely.

"How might we reduce the friction of group coordination and social validation in event planning, so that users never need to leave the app to do the social work that makes events actually happen?"

Reframed problem statement — driven by research

This shift is significant: better search is a UI problem. Group coordination and social identity are system design problems. They require features that feel less like tools and more like social infrastructure.

Design

The Reasoning Behind Every Key Decision

Before touching screens I defined three design principles from the research — and one structural constraint that shaped the entire IA: the same user could be both an attendee and a host, sometimes simultaneously.

Dual Persona Constraint

The app serves two distinct users — the Attendee discovering and coordinating, and the Host creating and managing. Both needed a coherent experience within a single account. Every feature had to work cleanly in both modes without requiring two separate apps.

1

Keep the group together.

Any coordination that happens off-platform is a product failure. The app needs to make group planning feel less chaotic than a group chat. Why this principle: 70% of users were already going off-platform mid-flow — the design had to eliminate the reason to leave.

2

Earn relevance, don't assume it.

Surfacing irrelevant events erodes trust faster than no recommendations at all. Personalization needs to feel intentional — collected upfront, not inferred silently. Why this principle: The 65% personalisation complaint came from users feeling manipulated, not helped, by existing algorithms.

3

Let users build an identity.

Social proof isn't vanity — it's how people decide who to trust and what to try. The profile isn't an afterthought; it's a social signal and a record of lived experience. Why this principle: 80% of users wanted event history displayed socially. No competitor was designing for this.

Navigation as a Design Statement

Placing the Create action at the centre of the bottom navigation — between Calendar and Map — was a deliberate statement: hosting is not a power-user feature. It's a first-class action available to anyone at any time. This directly addressed the finding that 70% of users found coordination difficult, by making event creation feel as accessible as browsing.

Grounding Filter

Every design decision was tested against one question: does this reduce the need to go off-platform? If a feature didn't reduce that need, it either needed to be redesigned or deprioritized.

Solution

The Three Problems, Solved

Problem 01

Clutter and Irrelevant Discovery

The Profile Preferences system lets users define their interests upfront during onboarding — so recommendations are earned rather than guessed. The Explore page surfaces two parallel discovery paths: "Events You're Invited to" (social, warm) above "Explore by Neighbourhood" (geo-based, cold) — prioritizing social context over algorithmic suggestion.

Discovery screens — Event Details · RSVP flow · Map with event pins · Filters panel

Design Rationale

The filter panel covers 5 dimensions (distance, date, category, price, social context) where existing platforms offered at most 2. Each dimension maps directly to a friction identified in the competitor audit.

Problem 02

The Coordination Gap

The Event Details page surfaces network data directly: attendee avatars, follow-status badges, and tickets remaining — answering "is anyone I know going?" without requiring a group chat. The RSVP flow is three steps: confirm guest count → confirm attendance and contact preference → registration success with ticket confirmation.

Event Details — 151 attendees · 49 tickets remaining · Network context · RSVP flow

Key Insight

Surfacing who from your network is attending — on the event detail page itself — eliminated the primary reason users abandoned events mid-planning. No separate "friends going" feature needed.

Problem 03

Identity and Social Validation

The Profile displays three social stats as equals: Following, Followers, and Events attended — treating event participation as social currency on par with relationships. My Events uses three tabs: Event Tickets (QR codes for physical check-in), Saved Events, and Hosted Events (with inline editing).

Identity screens — Onboarding preferences · Profile with event stats · Followers list · Profile edit

Design Rationale

The Following list appends event context to each connection: "Going pool party on 9/12." This turns the follower list into a live social feed of what people you know are doing — addressing the coordination gap without a separate feature.

What Didn't Work

Four Honest Failures

These aren't edge cases — they were real problems that required real changes. Hiding them would make this case study untrustworthy.

Failure 01 — RSVP Status Readability

The initial RSVP tracking used subtle status indicators. In testing, 4 of 10 users couldn't accurately state whether a friend had confirmed or was pending. Visually clean. Functionally ambiguous.

→ Redesigned with distinct color, iconography, and explicit labels so status was readable at a glance, not deduced.

Failure 02 — Over-animated UI

The first pass at animations was too aggressive. Users described the interface as "busy" and "distracting." The UI was competing with the social content it was meant to surface.

→ Rule I landed on: motion should confirm an action, not announce it. Pulled back intensity significantly.

Failure 03 — Showcase Was Too Passive

The initial event history auto-populated everything. Users felt they had no control over their own social identity — the feature felt like surveillance, not expression.

→ Expanded to a three-tab My Events system with explicit curation controls: Event Tickets, Saved Events, Hosted Events.

Failure 04 — Create Event as a Single Long Scroll

Hosts lost orientation mid-form (image, title, timing, location, description, social links, capacity, category, co-host, visibility, RSVP, dietary, cancellation policy — all at once). Completion rate was 60%.

→ Split into 4-step progressive disclosure. Completion improved to 91% in retesting.

What's Next

Where This Could Go

The current design solves the coordination and identity problems at the interaction level. There are two higher-order problems worth pursuing:

Group consensus before commitment

The hardest part of planning an event with friends isn't tracking RSVPs — it's reaching consensus on what to attend. A vote-on-options or shared shortlisting feature would target that upstream friction more directly than anything in the current design.

Behavioral instrumentation

Usability testing captured task completion, but not where users slowed down within a flow. Building behavioral analytics into the prototype earlier would surface which micro-decisions create friction before the final round of testing.

Host-side research depth

Most research participants were attendees. With dedicated host-side research, the Create Event flow — particularly visibility and capacity settings — would likely be structured differently. The host experience was underserved relative to what it deserves.

Reflection

What this project clarified

Functional problems and emotional problems require different design responses.

Finding events is a functional problem — addressable with better filters and cleaner UI. Coordination anxiety and the desire to be seen as someone with an interesting life are emotional problems — they require features that feel less like tools and more like social infrastructure. I went in expecting to design a better search experience. I came out having designed a system for how people show up together in their social lives.

The journey map was the turning point, not a deliverable.

The scope of the design opportunity became undeniable only when I mapped emotion across the full Before/During/After arc. It showed that the real opportunity wasn't in the app experience during the event — it was in everything that happened before and everything users wanted to carry away after. Without that map, the reframe doesn't happen and I design a better version of what already exists.

If I ran this again, I'd instrument the prototype earlier.

I ran usability testing on task completion rates but had no metrics on where users slowed down within a flow. That behavioral data would have told me which micro-decisions were causing friction before the final round — and would have given me evidence rather than instinct when making tradeoffs under time pressure.
