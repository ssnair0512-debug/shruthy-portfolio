Feature Design Decision Systems

WHY.

When teams have less time relitigating old decisions,
they have more time to make new ones.

The Real Problem

Companies at Relay's scale were becoming 2–5× slower because they kept revisiting decisions they'd already made. Teams would rebuild the same system twice because nobody remembered why the original approach was chosen. The reasoning had evaporated.

There wasn't a single source of truth for decisions — and no one had a system for capturing them in the moment they were made.

The problems were consistent across every team I spoke with:

  • No way to find a decision after the fact, or understand why it was made
  • Decisions lived in Slack threads, meeting notes, and people's heads
  • New engineers inherited choices with no context — and had no way to get it

I ran a deep-dive across 13 directions over 3 timing shapes. Individual contributors, managers, and lead stakeholders all described the same pattern: the decision happens fast, and the reasoning disappears faster. Before this project, several internal attempts had been made — none had shipped.

Research synthesis  ·  Affinity mapping  ·  13 interviews

Finding the Fix

Every existing tool I audited shared the same fatal flaw: they required a context switch. You had to stop what you were doing, open a wiki, fill in a template, and then remember to link it back to the conversation. Nobody did this consistently — especially not under time pressure.

My bet: the tool had to live where the decision happened, not two tabs away.

I identified three distinct moments when teams naturally pause after making a decision: in-thread (right after alignment), post-meeting (within 10 minutes of wrapping), and async-retrospective (when someone needs to explain past reasoning). Each timing shape needed a different entry point.

  • Round 1 — Slack bot that intercepted decision signals and prompted logging. Tested with two teams. Turned off by day three (too interruptive).
  • Round 2 — Passive digest. Better, but nobody read it. Discoverability wasn't the problem.
  • Round 3 — Persistent, numbered, org-visible ledger. 30-second capture. Fixed format: what, why, who, when.
Prototype  ·  Slack capture flow  ·  Iteration 2

What Actually Happened

The bot-driven model failed faster than I expected — and that was useful. 40 prompts in the first day. Everything turned off by day three. The signal was clear: interruption was worse than the problem we were solving.

The digest approach had a subtler failure. People checked it once, found it interesting, and never went back. The friction wasn't in the logging — it was in the habit formation. Nobody builds a habit around a digest.

The ledger model worked because it removed both failure modes. No interruptions during work. No separate inbox to maintain. You write a decision into the ledger when you're ready, and it's there when anyone needs it — searchable, linkable, numbered.

  • "It doesn't interrupt me — it's just there when I need it"
  • "I stopped asking 'why did we do this' in every standup"
  • "New engineers actually read these before asking questions"

What Changed

Three weeks after launching Why. with the Relay team, the behaviour shifted. New engineers started linking decisions in PR descriptions. Post-mortems referenced specific ledger entries by number. The most common feedback was not about the feature — it was about what they'd been missing.

  • 60+ decisions logged in the first 3 weeks without any prompting or mandate
  • 4 teams adopted it across the org without being asked
  • Zero support requests or friction complaints in the capture flow

The key insight: low-friction beats comprehensive. A decision with four fields that actually gets logged beats a 12-field template that sits empty. The format was the feature.

What I Had to Work With

No direct access to most stakeholders. Which questions surface required working against constraints without reworking the core infrastructure — everything had to sit on top of existing Postgres tables and the Slack integration already in place.

A real design system, available and fully documented. The new surface had to feel native to it from day one — no new patterns, no new components without strong justification.

An N of 1 available for co-design. Only a single power user accessible for live sessions. All broader signal came through interview notes and async feedback loops.

No direct access to the engineering backlog.

Prioritisation decisions had to be made with incomplete information. Everything shipped had to be reversible or additive — no breaking changes to existing patterns.

A real design system available.

Full component library in production. The new capture surface had to slot in without introducing new primitives — a significant constraint on visual exploration.

An N of 1 co-design partner.

Testing relied heavily on a single internal advocate. Validation cycles were longer, and I had to be more deliberate about when to observe versus when to ask.

What I'd Do Differently

I'd run the usability tests earlier — and with real decisions, not hypotheticals. The bot-driven approach failed not because the concept was wrong, but because I tested it with prompts that didn't carry the emotional weight of an actual architectural call. Real stakes surface real behaviour.

I'd invest more time in the search and retrieval layer from the start. The ledger works as a write-only tool today. But the value compounds when you can retrieve fast — by date, by team, by topic, by outcome. That surface deserved more design attention than the timeline allowed.

I'd also document the negative space better. I explored 13 directions before converging. Most of those directions were discarded quickly, and the reasoning for why only exists in my notes. That context should have been more legible to the team.

Retrospective  ·  What a second pass would look like

What I Learned

Capture is a product problem. Teams don't fail to document decisions because they're lazy — they fail because the act of documentation is structurally disconnected from the act of deciding. Fix the timing and the format, and behaviour follows without a mandate.

I also learned that visible numbers matter more than I expected. The fact that decisions were numbered — #45, #46, #47 — gave the ledger momentum and made entries citable in conversation. "We made this call — it's #47" is a sentence that only exists because the format made it natural.

The best knowledge system is the one that asks for the least while delivering the most. Why. taught me to design for the moment of minimum friction, not the moment of maximum detail.
