WUD! — Reimagining the Social Event Lifecycle

Event planning is social. The tools people use to plan events aren't. Users were already juggling Eventbrite for discovery, WhatsApp for coordination, Google Calendar for tracking, and Instagram for social proof — and none of these talked to each other. WUD! was the connective tissue that was missing.

Context

The Brief I Was Given — and the One I Chose to Answer

Design an intuitive event app that simplifies discovery, planning, and participation. That brief had an assumption baked in — that discovery was the problem. Before designing anything, I wanted to know if that was actually true.

The brief didn't specify whether "planning" meant the host's experience or the attendee's. That ambiguity turned out to be central to the design — and resolving it was the first design decision I made.

Starting assumption

The biggest barrier was discoverability — users couldn't find relevant events. This turned out to be only partially true.

Research

Findings

What the Data Actually Said

The discovery problem was real, but solvable. The coordination problem was where the experience truly broke down. The 80% identity statistic was the biggest surprise: users weren't just trying to attend events. They were trying to be seen as people who do interesting things. No existing platform was designing for that need.

Reframe

From "Better Search" to a Human Problem

Original brief

"Design an intuitive event app that simplifies discovery, planning, and participation."

Reframed HMW

"How might we reduce the friction of group coordination and social validation in event planning, so that users never need to leave the app to do the social work that makes events actually happen?"

How Might We

"How might we reduce the friction of group coordination and social validation in event planning, so that users never need to leave the app to do the social work that makes events actually happen?"

The original brief asked for better event discovery. The reframe changed the target entirely — from solo discovery to group participation, from finding events to making attendance feel socially safe and practically frictionless.

Design

Design Principles I Anchored To

Before touching screens, I defined three principles grounded directly in the research — and one structural constraint that shaped the entire IA: the same user could be both attendee and host simultaneously.

1

Keep the group together.

Any coordination that happens off-platform is a product failure. The app needs to make group planning feel less chaotic than a group chat.

2

Earn relevance, don't assume it.

Surfacing irrelevant events erodes trust faster than having no recommendations at all. Personalization must feel intentional — collected upfront, not inferred silently.

3

Let users build an identity.

Social proof isn't vanity — it's how people decide who to trust. The profile isn't an afterthought; it's a social signal and a record of lived experience.

Grounding filter

Every design decision was tested against one question: does this reduce the need to go off-platform?

Dual persona

The app serves two users: the Attendee discovering and coordinating, and the Host creating and managing — within a single account.

Process

What Failed, and What Changed

Two prototype rounds, 10 users each. Three core tasks: discover an event, coordinate attendance with friends, create and manage an event as a host.

What We Got Wrong First — RSVP status

The initial RSVP tracking UI used subtle status indicators that 4 of 10 users regularly misread. The design was visually clean but functionally ambiguous — users couldn't tell if a friend had confirmed or was still pending.

→
What changed

Redesigned RSVP states with distinct color, iconography, and explicit label — status readable at a glance, not deduced.

What We Got Wrong Second — Animation overload

The first pass at animations was too aggressive. Users described the interface as "busy" and "distracting." The UI was competing with the social content it was meant to surface.

→
What changed

Pulled back animation intensity significantly. The rule I landed on: motion should confirm an action, not announce it.

Create Event — form abandonment

The creation form was a single long scroll. Hosts lost orientation mid-form and abandoned before completing. Completion rate: 60%.

→
What changed

Split into a 4-step progressive disclosure flow with a persistent progress indicator. Step completion improved from 60% to 91% in retesting.

Solution

The Three Problems, Solved

Problem 01

Clutter and Irrelevant Discovery

Profile Preferences lets users define interests upfront during onboarding — so recommendations are earned rather than guessed. The Explore page surfaces two discovery paths: social invitations (warm) above neighbourhood events (cold), prioritizing social context over algorithmic suggestion.

Key feature

Profile Preferences — users define interests upfront so every recommendation is earned, not guessed.

Note

The filter panel covers 5 dimensions: Distance, Date, Category, Price range, and Social filters — each mapping to a gap identified in the competitor audit.

Problem 02

The Coordination Gap

Event Details surfaces network data directly: attendee avatars, follow-status badges, and tickets remaining — answering "is anyone I know going?" without requiring a group chat. The RSVP flow is three steps and ends with a clear confirmation state that signals the coordination is complete.

Key insight

Surfacing who from your network is attending — on the event detail page itself — eliminated the primary reason users abandoned events mid-planning.

Problem 03

Identity and Social Validation

Profile displays Following, Followers, and Events Attended as equal social stats — treating event participation as social currency on par with relationships. My Events uses a three-tab system: Event Tickets (QR codes for physical check-in), Saved Events, and Hosted Events.

Key insight

80% of users wanted social event history. No existing platform was designing for this need.

Reflection

What I'd Do Differently

Go deeper on group consensus dynamics

The coordination features I designed addressed the problem at the interaction level. Looking back, the hardest part of planning with friends isn't tracking RSVPs — it's reaching consensus on what to attend. A vote-on-options or shared shortlisting tool would have targeted that friction more directly.

Instrument the prototype earlier

I ran usability testing on task completion, but I didn't have metrics on where users slowed down within a flow. That behavioral data would have surfaced micro-friction before the final round of testing.

Give the host experience equal research weight

Most of my interview participants were event attendees. With more host-side research, the Create Event flow might have been structured differently — particularly visibility and capacity settings, which hosts found most difficult to configure.

Limitation

Host-side experience deprioritized. Event creation designed late and tested in only one round.

If I ran this again

Instrument earlier, go deeper on group consensus dynamics, and test host flows with equal rigor to attendee flows.

Takeaway

The Bigger Lesson

This project taught me that functional problems and emotional problems require different design responses.

The functional problem — finding events — was addressable with better filters and a cleaner UI. The emotional problem — coordination anxiety and the desire to be seen as someone with an interesting life — required features that felt less like tools and more like social infrastructure.

The journey map was the turning point. Mapping emotion across Before/During/After made the scope of the problem undeniable — the design opportunity wasn't in the app experience itself, it was in everything that happened at the edges of it.

Core lesson

Functional problems and emotional problems require different design responses. Don't mistake one for the other.
